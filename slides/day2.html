  • Measure agreement between two reviewers, beyond chance
  • Turn disagreements into clearer checklist wording

Severity

Rate a finding by what it does to the decision, not by how you found it

3 · Critical

The decision changes, or the output cannot leave the unit

2 · Major

The size or the confidence of the result changes, not the decision

1 · Minor

Presentation: a label, a chart, a name

Recap from DS-310. Ranking flaws by consequence is the individual reviewer's skill. Today two reviewers must reach the same rating, and the unit's standard (S-5) writes the scale down.

The "or the output cannot leave the unit" clause is the unit's own rule: it makes a privacy finding critical whatever its effect on the decision. It is the one most reviewers forget.

Measuring the effect

Effect of slot booking on waiting at T2, hours per call (Lab 2.1 stretch). The draft recommends extending at -2 hours or more; after C1 it does not.

This is how severity stops being an opinion: re-run with the flaw fixed. C1 alone moves the effect from -5.36 to -1.37 and the decision changes: critical. C2 moves it further but does not flip it on its own: major.

Beyond chance

50% agreement is a weighted kappa of 0.25

0.25
weighted kappa, six findings (Lab 2.1)
  • Kappa removes the agreement two reviewers reach by chance
  • Weighted kappa counts a gap of two as worse than a gap of one
  • 1 is perfect agreement; 0 is no better than chance
  • Track it each quarter on a shared analysis

Cohen (1968) introduced weighted kappa for ordered ratings. With six findings the number is noisy: use it as a trend over quarters, not a grade for one review. The point for the room: half agreement sounds acceptable; corrected for chance it is weak.

Sources: Cohen (1968), Weighted kappa, Psychological Bulletin.

The output of calibration

Each big gap becomes a sentence in the checklist

P1, before

Is the output free of personal data, and is its classification marked?

P1, after

The same question, plus the anchor: a breach is critical whatever its effect on the decision, because the output cannot leave the unit.

Calibration that ends in a discussion is forgotten by next quarter. Calibration that ends in a changed sentence stays. Ask each pair for the sentence they would add for their own biggest gap.

Checkpoint

High agreement, low kappa: what happened?

Two reviewers rate 20 findings and agree on 16 of them, 80%. Their weighted kappa is 0.3. Which explanation fits?

The kappa was computed wrongly
Most findings were rated minor by both, so much of the agreement was expected by chance
Four disagreements are too many for any team
Twenty findings are too few to compute kappa
Why. When nearly everything is rated minor, two reviewers agree often by chance; kappa removes that, so the agreement on the rare serious findings decides it.

Answer: chance agreement on a skewed scale. The practical lesson: calibrate on analyses that hold serious flaws, where agreement matters.

Session 2

Auditing assisted work

  • Audit work against rules from its evidence, not its claims
  • Recompute a number an assistant drafted
  • Record each breach with the rule it breaks
02
Audit or review

A review asks if the analysis is right; an audit asks if the rules were followed

Review

One analysis, all five areas, before release. The reviewer re-runs and rates findings.

Audit

A sample of released work, against the rules for assisted analysis. The auditor checks the evidence each rule asks for.

The rule of thumb. No evidence, no compliance. "I checked the numbers" is a claim; a verification record is evidence.

The audit closes the loop on Day 1's rules: rules nobody audits become optional within a quarter. The catalog's evidence for outcome 3 is a rule set and an audit of five assisted analyses.

Five assisted analyses from the unit's log

Recompute what you can

           & (calls.month >= 13)]

From the Lab 2.2 solution run. An audit cannot recompute everything; it recomputes the headline of the analyses whose records are missing, because that is where an assistant's error survives.

A2 is the most serious even though its numbers were right: restricted data left the authority's control, which the national guidelines forbid and no later step undoes.

What the audit teaches

Two of five had no verification record: change the process, not just the analyses

  • The rule existed; nothing made it visible before review
  • Proposed: no review of assisted work starts without its verification record
  • The checklist item C2 asks for it at every level

How often

Five analyses a quarter, chosen at random from the assisted log, plus any the reviewers flag.

The sample size is a choice on the Director's behalf for the case; in a real unit it depends on volume. The point is that the audit is routine and random, not a reaction to an incident.

Tone

An audit finds breaches of rules, not bad analysts

Record facts

What the file shows, which rule, which action

Fix the process

Two missing records point at the process, not two people

Feed development

A pattern for one analyst goes into feedback, the next session

If an audit feels like a hunt, analysts stop disclosing assistant use, and the audit sees nothing. Keep the table factual and the follow-up developmental.

Checkpoint

"I checked the numbers"

An analyst's assisted analysis has a disclosure line and correct figures when you recompute them, but no prompts and no verification record. He says he checked everything. What does the audit record?

No breach: the figures are right
A breach of the disclosure rule
A breach of the keep rules: no prompts and no record
A breach of the verify rule only
Why. The keep rules ask for prompts and a verification record in the repository; their absence is the breach, however right the figures turn out to be.

Answer: the keep rules (A5 in the lab). Some will argue the right figures excuse it: ask how the next reviewer would know which numbers were checked.

Session 3

Feedback that builds skill

  • Choose the findings worth raising with an analyst
  • Write feedback that names the reasoning, not only the error
  • Plan development with goals, evidence and unaided practice
03
From review to feedback

Seven findings is a review; three is feedback

  • Shaden Al-Ruwayshid joined six months ago; Faleh found seven things in her notebook
  • The review notes go to the analysis; the feedback goes to the analyst
  • Choose the three findings that show a pattern in her reasoning
  • Name one strength, specifically

Her three

No comparison group (F2), the wrong population in an average (F1), assisted cells not verified (F7)

The minor findings (an axis, variable names) go in the review notes, not in the conversation. Three is a memory limit, not a rule of politeness.

One note

Observation, effect, reasoning, next step

PartShaden, F2
ObservationIn cell 5 waiting at T2 is compared before and after, with no other terminal
EffectWaiting fell everywhere in month 13, so most of the fall would have happened anyway
ReasoningBefore claiming a cause, ask what would have happened without it
NextIn your next comparison, write the comparison group in the first cell

The reasoning line is what builds skill: it names the question she did not ask herself, so she can ask it next time on a different problem. The catalog calls this feedback that names the reasoning.

Specific

"Be more careful with comparisons" tells her nothing she can do

Vague

Be more careful. Improve your rigour. Pay attention to the data. Good job on the charts.

Specific

Cell 3 averages imports with transshipment, 5.2 days. Print the counts by move type before every average.

The peer test. Would a colleague reading the note know what Shaden should do differently next week? That is the evidence of outcome 4.

The lab's check flags vague words and notes without a cell or a number. Praise needs the same specificity: "the chart in cell 7 is easy to read" teaches; "good job" does not.

04
Capacity

LevelAnalyses next quarterHours eachWho

31210a senior
4416 + 8a senior and a second reviewer

The hours are the unit's planning figures for the case, set on the Director's behalf; a real unit measures its own from last year's log. The point is that every level has a cost, so a standard without a rota is a wish.

The bottleneck

Seniors are needed for 184 hours and have 150

34 h
short in senior review hours next quarter
  • Three seniors, 60, 50 and 40 review hours
  • Six mid-level analysts have hours to spare
  • The rule keeps level 3 and 4 with seniors

From Lab 2.4. Ask the room for options before the next slide. The tempting one is to lower the level of a few analyses. That is the under-assurance that produced last year's errors.

Options

Close the gap without lowering a level

Co-sign

A mid-level analyst reviews a level 3 analysis; a senior checks the review in 2 hours. Saves 8 senior hours.

Defer

Move a level 3 analysis whose decision can wait to the next quarter, with the sponsor's agreement.

Never

Lower the level to fit the rota. The level comes from the risk, not the hours.

Five co-signed reviews close 34 hours at 8 each, the fewest that work. Co-signing is also training: it builds the next level 3 reviewers, the only lasting fix.

Mentoring

Juniors shadow a mentor's reviews before they review

  • Juniors do not review alone, but they watch reviews from their first month
  • One junior per mentor; the mentor reviews some of the junior's work when the rota allows
  • Shaden with Faleh; Turki with Lina; Dalia with Rakan

A trade-off to state

Faleh is at full capacity and mentors Shaden. Either the shadowing fits inside his reviews, or one of his reviews moves.

Shadowing costs no booked hours, but it does cost attention. The reference pairs put Shaden with Faleh because her plan's month 1 is pair review with him: check that the rota allows it.

Checkpoint

Senior hours run short: what do you do?

Next quarter's level 3 and 4 reviews need 184 senior hours; your seniors have 150. A director asks you to "just call some of them level 2".

Lower five level 3 analyses to level 2
Ask the seniors to absorb the extra hours
Let mid-level analysts review level 3 alone
Co-sign five level 3 reviews and defer what can wait
Why. The level comes from the risk; co-signing keeps a senior's judgement on each level 3 review at a fifth of the hours and trains the next reviewers.

Answer: co-sign and defer. Ask how they would answer the director: "the level comes from what an error would cost you, not from our diary".
